Flying with a GLP-1 pen: what the rules actually say

By Ümit Can Erim · Published 20 August 2026 · Updated 20 August 2026 · Sources checked 20 August 2026 · How these guides are written

The security part is more permissive than most people expect: medication is exempt from the 100 ml rule, and gel ice packs for keeping it cool are allowed even when they have melted. The part that actually catches people is not at the airport at all — it is the in-use clock that keeps running while you are away, and one product whose storage rule only goes one direction.

At the checkpoint, in TSA's own words

If you are flying from a US airport, three sentences cover almost all of it:

  • "You may bring medically necessary liquids, medications and creams in excess of 3.4 ounces or 100 milliliters in your carry-on bag. Remove them from your carry-on bag to be screened separately from the rest of your belongings."
  • "TSA allows larger amounts of medically necessary liquids, gels, and aerosols in reasonable quantities for your trip, but you must declare them to TSA officers at the checkpoint for inspection."
  • "Medically necessary gel ice packs in reasonable quantities are allowed regardless of their physical state of matter (e.g., melted or slushy)." Ordinary frozen items have to be frozen solid; medically necessary ones do not.

TSA also says: "It is recommended that medication be clearly labeled to facilitate the screening process." Not a requirement — but the pharmacy label is already on the box, so the easiest version of this is to travel with the carton rather than a loose pen. That happens to be what the storage instructions ask for anyway, since the carton is what protects the pen from light.

And the sentence worth remembering above the others, printed on every one of those pages: "The final decision rests with the TSA officer on whether an item is allowed through the checkpoint." The rules describe the normal case, not a guarantee.

Outside the United States

Rules differ by country and, in the UK right now, by airport — the liquids rules have been changing at different sites on different timetables. This page is not going to paraphrase a regulation it cannot quote in full, so: check the official page for where you are flying from, and check it again for the return leg, because the two ends of a trip are two different jurisdictions. For the UK that is GOV.UK's hand luggage restrictions. What is broadly consistent across them is the shape: medicines are treated as an exception to the liquid limits, and the exception is easier to use if you can show what the medicine is.

The one-way rule. Tirzepatide's instructions allow room temperature for up to 21 days if needed — and then say not to return it to the refrigerator once it has been stored that way. A hotel week is well inside 21 days, but putting the pen back in the fridge at home is the step the label rules out. Semaglutide pens are different: their in-use period explicitly allows either room temperature or the refrigerator. Which one you have decides whether the trip is reversible. The storage guide has the numbers for each.

The clock that runs while you are away

A pen in use has a day count attached to it, and a holiday does not pause it. Two questions are worth answering before you pack rather than at a pharmacy counter in another country:

  • Will this pen still be within its in-use window when the last dose of the trip is due? The count started the day you first used it, not the day you left.
  • Does the trip cross the point where you would normally start a new one? If so, the new pen travels too — and so does whatever it needs to stay cold until it is opened.

Both are answerable in a second if the opening date is written down somewhere, and awkward if it is not.

Time zones and the dose day

Crossing time zones moves the hour a weekly reminder lands relative to your body, though the calendar day is unchanged. The labels are relaxed about the hour — "at any time of day" appears in all of them — so the hour is yours to choose. The day is the part with a rule attached, and for the semaglutide products that rule is quoted in the reminders guide: the day may be changed as long as the last dose was two or more days before.

Hold luggage, and why not

TSA permits liquid medication in checked bags. The reasons not to are practical rather than regulatory: the hold is neither temperature-controlled in the way a cabin is nor guaranteed to arrive when you do, and a pen that has been through either problem is a pen you cannot verify. Keeping it with you also keeps it available if a connection turns into an overnight.

The opening date, and what is left in it

Doses remaining, days since opening and the expiry date on one card — so "will it last the trip" is a glance rather than a calculation. An afternoon out of the fridge can be logged as a storage event and stays on the vial's record.

Questions

Do I need a doctor's letter?

TSA does not require one; it recommends that medication be clearly labelled. Other countries set their own expectations, and some travellers carry a prescription copy simply because it ends a conversation quickly. Check the rules for the airport you are leaving from and the one you are coming back through.

Will the X-ray damage the pen?

TSA's guidance is to remove medically necessary liquids and present them separately for inspection; if you prefer they not be X-rayed, that is what asking for inspection at the checkpoint is for. Screening decisions rest with the officer.

Can I take a cool bag or ice pack?

TSA allows medically necessary gel ice packs in reasonable quantities regardless of state — melted or slushy is fine, which is the opposite of the rule for ordinary frozen items. Declare them with the medication.

What if the pen gets warm on the way?

The in-use instructions allow a period at room temperature, and the length differs by product. Whether a particular pen is still fit to use after a particular journey is not something a page can decide — that is a question for a pharmacist, who will ask how warm and for how long. Logging it while it happens is what lets you answer.

Should I move my dose day for the trip?

That is a question for your prescriber, and the labels set the boundary rather than the answer: for the semaglutide products, the day may be changed as long as the last dose was two or more days before. Other products may not carry the same clause.
