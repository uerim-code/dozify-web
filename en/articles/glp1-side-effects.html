Managing common GLP-1 side effects

Educational guide · Published June 24, 2026 · ~7 min read

Most people starting a GLP-1 medication notice some digestive side effects in the first weeks. The most common ones — nausea, an upset stomach and constipation — are usually mild and tend to ease over time. This guide explains why they happen, general comfort strategies, and the warning signs that mean it is time to seek medical care.

Why GLP-1 medications cause side effects

GLP-1 medications work in part by slowing how quickly your stomach empties, so food stays in the stomach longer and you feel full sooner. This is the intended mechanism, but the same slowing is behind most of the digestive side effects people notice early on.

Side effects are most common in the first couple of weeks and for a short period after each dose change. For most people they fade as the body adjusts. The notes below are general, educational information — they are not a treatment plan, and your pharmacist or doctor can tailor advice to you.

Common side effects

  • Nausea — the side effect people report most often. It tends to be strongest early on and after a dose increase, then settles.
  • General stomach upset — reflux, heartburn, bloating or a feeling of fullness that lasts longer than usual.
  • Constipation — slower digestion plus eating less (and sometimes drinking less) can make bowel movements less frequent.
  • Loss of appetite — eating smaller amounts is expected, but it can also mean you take in less fluid and fibre than before.

Comfort strategies for nausea and stomach upset

These are gentle, everyday adjustments many people find helpful. They are general tips, not medical instructions — if nausea is interfering with eating or drinking, talk to your pharmacist or doctor.

  • Eat smaller, more frequent meals rather than a few large ones.
  • Eat slowly and stop when you feel comfortably full.
  • Go easy on fatty, fried and very rich foods, which can sit heavily.
  • Sip fluids between meals rather than with large meals.
  • Stay upright for a while after eating instead of lying down.
  • Some people find plain, bland foods and ginger or peppermint tea soothing.

Staying hydrated and managing constipation

Because you may be eating and drinking less, hydration and fibre matter more than usual. General comfort tips include:

  • Drink fluids regularly through the day, even when you are not very thirsty.
  • Include fibre-rich foods — fruit, vegetables, whole grains and legumes — within what you can comfortably eat.
  • Gentle daily movement, such as a short walk, can help keep things regular.

If constipation persists, or if you are thinking about a fibre supplement, laxative or any other over-the-counter product, ask your pharmacist or doctor first — they can tell you whether it is suitable for you, especially if you are also feeling nauseated or struggling to drink enough. This page does not recommend specific products or doses.

When to seek medical care

Most side effects are mild, but some symptoms need prompt attention. If you experience any of the following, contact a healthcare professional right away, or call your local emergency number (in Türkiye, dial 112):

  • Severe abdominal pain, especially pain high in the belly that spreads to your back and does not ease.
  • Persistent vomiting, or being unable to keep fluids down.
  • Signs of dehydration — dizziness or fainting when standing, very little or no urine, or confusion.
  • Signs of a serious allergic reaction — swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat, or trouble breathing.
  • A swollen, hard belly with an inability to pass stool or gas, particularly alongside vomiting.
  • Vomit or stool that looks bloody or black.

Trust your judgement: if something feels seriously wrong, do not wait — seek care.

A note on who should be cautious

GLP-1 medications are not suitable for everyone. They carry a boxed warning and are not used in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) or Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2). Tell your doctor about any such history before starting, and report a new lump or swelling in the neck, persistent hoarseness, or difficulty swallowing. Your doctor and pharmacist are the right people to decide whether this medication is appropriate for you.
