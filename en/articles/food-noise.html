Food noise on a GLP-1: what people mean, and how to track it

By Ümit Can Erim · Published 9 October 2026 · Updated 9 October 2026 · Sources checked 9 October 2026 · How these guides are written

"Food noise" is a patient's phrase that researchers are now trying to pin down: persistent thoughts about food that feel unwanted, that keep coming back and that are different from ordinary hunger. People taking GLP-1 medicines often say it goes quieter, and that is one reason the term spread. What has not been measured is whether it rises and falls with your own dosing week. That part you can watch for yourself, as long as you log it in a way that can be read later.

Read this first. Food noise is not a diagnosis, and this page does not assess anyone. If thoughts about food are distressing, or eating feels out of your control, that is worth raising with your clinician, whatever medicine you take.

Where the term comes from

It did not start in a textbook. A 2023 review in Nutrients by Hayashi and colleagues describes "food noise" as a colloquial term, spreading through reports from patients and clinicians on GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide, who described less rumination and less obsessive preoccupation with food. The authors tie it to an established research idea, food cue reactivity: the way the sight, smell or even the sound of food triggers thoughts and cravings. They offer a working description: heightened or persistent food cue reactivity that often leads to intrusive thoughts about food and to eating patterns people do not want.

A 2025 paper in Nutrition & Diabetes by Dhurandhar and colleagues proposes a more formal definition: persistent thoughts about food that the person perceives as unwanted and/or dysphoric, and that may cause harm, including social, mental or physical problems. What separates it from ordinary food thoughts, they write, is intensity and intrusiveness, closer to rumination than to planning dinner.

It is not the same as hunger

The Hayashi review makes a point worth keeping in mind when you log it: responses to food cues, and the cravings that come with them, can show up regardless of how physically hungry you are. You can have eaten an hour ago and still have a head full of food. That is why it is worth rating food noise separately from hunger, rather than folding both into one "appetite" score.

How researchers measure it

The Food Noise Questionnaire, published in Obesity in 2025 by Diktas and colleagues, has five statements answered on a five-point agree-to-disagree scale, added into one score. One design choice is useful for anyone tracking at home: participants answer about the last two weeks, specifically so that their answer is not swayed by brief situations when food noise happened to be high or low. In other words, the researchers expected it to move around from day to day. The authors also say that clinically meaningful cut-offs, and how well the score responds to treatment, are still unknown.

Why it may move across your week

The model in the Hayashi review lists things that change how strongly someone reacts to food cues, split into constant factors (such as genetics and usual food preferences) and transient ones: time of day, sleep, stress, emotional state, physical activity, and levels of appetite-regulating hormones, including GLP-1 medicines. So a rating that changes from Monday to Thursday is not surprising, and a bad night's sleep or a hard week at work may explain as much as your dose day does.

Some people on weekly injections describe food noise as quieter in the days after the dose and louder before the next one. That is a lived-experience report, and it may well be true for them; but none of the studies cited here tracked food noise day by day across a dosing week, so there is no published pattern to compare yourself against. Treat what you see in your own log as information about you, not as a finding about the medicine. And if a pattern does show up, it is something to bring to your prescriber, not a reason to move your dose day or change the dose on your own.

How to log it so it means something later

  • Same time, same scale. Rate it at roughly the same time of day, and do not change what the numbers mean halfway through. Time of day is one of the transient factors in the model above.
  • Keep it separate from hunger. Two short ratings tell you more than one blended one.
  • Add one line of context when something unusual happened: little sleep, a stressful day, illness, travel.
  • Log the dose as usual, with its real date. The comparison only works if the dose history is accurate.
  • Wait before you judge. One week is an anecdote; several weeks of ratings start to show whether anything repeats.

When to bring it up

The Dhurandhar definition includes harm for a reason: for some people food noise brings distress and self-blame along with it. If that is you, or if you notice yourself restricting hard, losing control around food, or dreading meals, tell your clinician. Questions

Is food noise a medical diagnosis?

No. It is a term that came from patients and is now being defined and measured by researchers. The questionnaire's authors say clinically meaningful cut-offs have not been established.

Does Ozempic® or another GLP-1 make food noise go away?

Many patients report that it gets quieter, which is how the term became widely known, and researchers are studying it. The reviews cited here describe those reports as anecdotal so far and call for systematic research. Nobody can promise what you will experience.

It comes back before my next injection. Should I change my dose day?

Not on your own. Bring your log to your prescriber: dated ratings next to dated doses are exactly the kind of detail that helps them decide whether anything should change.

How often should I rate it?

Often enough to see a week's shape, at a time you can keep to. The research questionnaire looks back over two weeks; a short daily or every-other-day rating answers a different question, whether it moves within the week.

Is food noise the same as cravings?

They overlap. The Hayashi review links food noise to food cue reactivity, which includes cravings; the Dhurandhar definition stresses persistent, unwanted thoughts. Researchers are still debating where one ends and the other begins.
